## The three that matter most

### Partee — a second Bergey, and a full salary schedule

Dennis Partee, Chargers. The opinion states his contract **year by year**:

    1974   $38,500
    1975   $44,000
    1976   $49,500

**And the WFL offered him $50,000 for a single 1974 season** — more than his
Chargers salary that year, and the basis of the antitrust damages ($34,500 actual,
trebled to $103,500; contract damages $30,550 separately).

**This is the Bergey shape again**: one man, one year, two leagues, from a neutral
finder of fact. Two such cases is no longer an anomaly — it is a **method**. The
WFL and USFL litigation is where the reserve system's price gets measured, because
that is exactly what the plaintiffs had to prove.

### Rudolph — four contracts in one workers'-compensation case

    Ed Rudolph      $65,000 per season (Tampa Bay, assigned to Miami);
                    grievance award $15,000
    Floyd Wells     $27,000 per year, wide receiver, signed 22 May 1980,
                    payable "if he made the team roster"
    Bill Windauer   $35,000 (1977) and $39,000 (1978), two-year deal, base salary

**Wells's clause is the roster-bonus condition in plain language** — the same
structure as Smith's "$5,000 if he made the team", fifteen years later.

Also: the Dolphins were **self-insured for workers' compensation up to $100,000**,
with excess policies above — a club-finance fact, not a salary.

### Heidel — a 1965 signing bonus, three years earlier than anything else held

James B. Heidel, St. Louis / Chicago Cardinals. Received **20 December 1965**:

    $50,000   bonus
    $ 5,000   additional compensation
    $ 1,000   travel funds

And the Tax Court **quotes the contract clause**: *"the club agrees to pay the
player the sum of $50,000.00. By the execution hereof, the player acknowledges
receipt of said sum…"*

**This pushes the itemised-contract record back from 1968 to 1965.** And a $50,000
signing bonus in 1965 sits well above Smith's $23,000 in 1968 — worth noting, not
explaining, since Heidel signed out of the AFL–NFL bidding war and Smith did not.

**Tax Court is a source class worth pursuing.** These cases exist *because* the
figures were disputed, so they are itemised, dated, and quoted from the
instrument. Gardin is the same class: **$43,326 total football earnings for 1971**,
stipulated.

## Brown v. Pro Football — structural, not per-player

Only one figure, seven times: on **17 May 1989 the Executive Committee of the NFL
Management Council agreed to pay developmental squad players a fixed salary of
$1,000 per week.**

That is not a contract, it is **a league-wide wage set unilaterally** — the same
class of fact as Smith's option-year rule (90% of the prior year's salary). Both
belong as **structural claims about the system**, not as salary claims about a
person.

## What scan two changes

**The WFL/USFL litigation is a systematic source, not a lucky find.** Bergey,
Greenwood, Beathard, Partee and the USFL Players Association are five cases in
which a rival league's pay is compared against NFL pay, or a club's failure to pay
is adjudicated — because that comparison *was the legal question*. That is a
reproducible search, not a lucky dip.

**Tax Court is a second such vein.** Heidel and Gardin both itemise a player's
income because the itemisation was the dispute.

**And two structural rules are now in hand** — the option year at 90% of prior
salary (Smith, 1968) and the developmental squad at $1,000/week (Brown, 1989).
Both are claims about the **system**, and under the salary-convention rule neither
is a `salary_*` predicate at all.
